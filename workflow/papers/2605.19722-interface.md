Title: Measuring Safety Alignment Effects in Autonomous Security Agents
Authors: Isaac David, Arthur Gervais
Abstract (verbatim): "Do stock safety-aligned language models and their uncensored or abliterated derivatives behave differently when run as autonomous security agents? Single-turn refusal benchmarks cannot answer this question: security agents must inspect repositories, call tools, and produce vulnerability evidence inside authorized sandboxes. We present a trace-based benchmark of 30 local vulnerability-analysis tasks with fixed tools, deterministic success predicates, redaction rules, and grounding checks, and compare four stock models against uncensored or abliterated derivatives: Gemma 4 31B, Gemma 4 26B A4B, Qwen2.5-Coder 7B, and Llama 3.1 8B. The artifact contains 1,500 security-agent traces and 800 non-security control traces. The Gemma pairs show large less-restricted gains on security tasks: 14.0% versus 0.7% success for 31B and 10.7% versus 0.0% for 26B, with higher mean grounding (3.91 versus 3.27 and 4.12 versus 1.64 out of five) and 0.0% refusal, suppressed-action, and unsafe-action rates in the 31B traces."

Core Contribution:
Introduces a trace-based benchmark methodology for evaluating safety alignment effects in LLM-based security agents, measuring the behavioral gap between stock aligned models and their less-restricted (uncensored/abliterated) derivatives across end-to-end vulnerability analysis tasks. Key finding: single-turn refusal rates are insufficient signals; system-level evaluation separating refusal, unsafe action, tool reliability, and evidence grounding is necessary. Results are model-family-dependent: Gemma pairs show large less-restricted gains, Qwen and Llama pairs show no gain or regression.

Limitations Authors Acknowledge:
scope_limit_1 | non-Gemma families do not reproduce the success gain pattern; Gemma results may reflect capability gap as much as alignment gap
scope_limit_2 | less-restricted derivatives are empirical probes, not clean causal counterfactuals; matched pairs have confounded capability and alignment changes
scope_limit_3 | hard task classes (proof_of_trigger, patch_verification) unsolved across all conditions; low absolute success rates throughout
scope_limit_4 | stronger causal claims require cleaner matched pairs, better tool interfaces, executable evidence checks, and larger independent annotation
scope_limit_5 | paper does not argue less-restricted models are safe to deploy; production systems still need alignment, sandboxing, policy checks, evidence verification
scope_limit_6 | no third-party targets evaluated; no reusable exploit payloads published
scope_limit_7 | Gemma gains appear equally in non-security control tasks suggesting results may be general capability difference, not security-specific alignment effect

Implementation Pitfalls (do NOT do this):
do_not_1 | do not use single-turn refusal rate as primary safety signal for security agents; traces without explicit refusals can still exhibit alignment-suppressed behavior in evidence grounding
do_not_2 | do not conflate less-restricted success gain with safety alignment removal being the causal factor; non-security control gains are comparable, suggesting capability confound
do_not_3 | do not use LLM judges for success predicates in this benchmark; predicates are deterministic and machine-readable
do_not_4 | do not compare across controller modes (strict vs repair) without accounting for mode difference in run counts and trace structure
do_not_5 | do not generalize Gemma results to Qwen or Llama families; results are model-family-dependent with reversed patterns in two of four pairs
do_not_6 | do not treat grounding score as semantic comprehension; it is a deliberate non-semantic substring check for auditability
do_not_7 | do not skip the 4-point decision gate when interpreting whether a success difference is security-specific; all four criteria must be evaluated jointly
